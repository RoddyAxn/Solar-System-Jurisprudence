SOLAR SYSTEM JURISPRUDENCE

VOLUME VIII

CHAPTER 87

THE NATIONAL POLICY SUITE

A SOLAR SYSTEM JURISPRUDENCE PROGRAMME FOR AOTEAROA NEW ZEALAND

Roderick J. Young, B.Sc., B.Soc.Sc.(Hons), P.G. Dip. (Public Policy), LLB.

I. PURPOSE

Chapter 86 identified the Purchased Silence Problem: the risk that concentrated money may bend the national information environment without any express command, conspiracy or provable act of censorship.

The present chapter proposes a practical policy suite for Aotearoa New Zealand.

It does not proceed from the proposition that every corporation is corrupt, every media organisation is captured, every public servant is compromised or every market transaction is illegitimate.

It proceeds from a more disciplined proposition:

Where power has gravitational mass, the law must measure the field, make the field visible and protect those who would otherwise be pulled silently into its orbit.

Solar System Jurisprudence treats society as a system of interacting bodies.

Citizens, corporations, government departments, councils, courts, broadcasters, banks, pharmaceutical companies, gambling operators, landlords, tenants, iwi, hapū, families and future generations do not operate in isolation.

Each creates effects upon the others.

A policy is therefore incomplete when it counts the immediate transaction but ignores the wider orbit of consequences.

The policy suite proposed in this chapter rests on seven principles:

1. informational sovereignty;

2. transparent structural gravity;

3. intergenerational trusteeship;

4. social floor protection;

5. polluter and beneficiary responsibility;

6. distributed civic power; and

7. evidence before accusation.

II. CONSTITUTIONAL FOUNDATION

New Zealand law already recognises freedom of expression, including the freedom to seek, receive and impart information and opinions of any kind in any form.[1]

Solar System Jurisprudence builds upon that protection.

Freedom to speak is not sufficient where some voices possess millions of dollars of amplification and others possess none.

The law should not determine the correct opinion.

It should expose the financial and institutional forces acting upon the channels through which opinions are formed.

The proposed constitutional principle is:

Every person has a civic interest in receiving sufficient information to understand the material economic, institutional and environmental forces surrounding a significant public decision.

This principle would be called the Right to an Informed National Voice.

It would not create a judicial power to order newspapers to publish particular views.

It would instead support disclosure of material conflicts; open government information; transparency of media revenue; visibility of lobbying; public-interest research; accessible regulatory data; protection of journalists and whistleblowers; and reasoned explanations for major public decisions.

The existing right to freedom of expression under s 14 of the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act 1990 should remain central.[2]

Any restriction on commercial advertising would need to be prescribed by law, demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society and designed no more broadly than necessary.

A ban on direct-to-consumer advertising of prescription medicines should therefore be framed as a restriction upon product promotion, not upon scientific debate, health education, journalism or patient access to reliable medical information.

III. POLICY ONE: THE INFORMED NATIONAL VOICE ACT

Parliament should enact an Informed National Voice Act.

The Act would establish a statutory framework for understanding structural influence without empowering the Government to dictate editorial conclusions.

A. Objects

The objects would be:

1. to protect the practical conditions required for informed democratic participation;

2. to disclose material financial influence over public communications;

3. to strengthen public confidence in journalism and public decision-making;

4. to improve access to public-interest information; and

5. to preserve freedom of expression and editorial independence.

B. Public-interest information duties

Specified public bodies would be required to publish, in accessible form:

- the evidence relied upon for major policy decisions;

- conflicts of interest;

- material meetings with commercial interests;

- regulatory impact statements;

- significant modelling assumptions;

- public asset valuations;

- major procurement exemptions;

- public-benefit and distributional assessments; and

- post-implementation reviews.

C. Duty to state uncertainty

A Minister, regulator or department presenting a forecast should distinguish observed fact; modelled estimate; assumption; policy preference; and unresolved uncertainty.

This would not punish ordinary political argument.

It would require official documents to show which propositions are measured and which are inferred.

D. Public reasoning standard

For any decision exceeding a statutory fiscal, environmental or strategic threshold, the responsible authority would publish a Statement of Public Reasoning explaining:

1. the problem;

2. the evidence;

3. the available alternatives;

4. the expected beneficiaries;

5. the expected cost-bearers;

6. effects on future generations;

7. Māori rights and interests;

8. regional effects;

9. environmental consequences; and

10. how success will be measured.

IV. POLICY TWO: PRESCRIPTION MEDICINES ADVERTISING REFORM

New Zealand law currently permits advertising of prescription medicines subject to the Medicines Act 1981 and the Medicines Regulations 1984. The Regulations require prescription-medicine advertisements to carry specified information, including identification that the product is a prescription medicine.[3]

Government policy work on replacement medical-products legislation continued through 2025, including Cabinet consideration of advertising controls.[4]

Solar System Jurisprudence proposes a clearer rule.

A. General prohibition

It should be unlawful to advertise a named prescription medicine directly to the general public for the purpose of increasing consumer demand.

The prohibition should cover television; radio; newspapers and magazines; outdoor advertising; paid internet advertising; social-media promotion; paid search placement; sponsored content; paid influencers; product-linked disease-awareness campaigns; and personalised digital targeting.

B. Permitted communications

The prohibition would not apply to Medsafe safety notices; Pharmac information; Ministry of Health campaigns; independent patient-information services; legally required consumer medicine information; peer-reviewed scientific publications; communications directed to authorised health practitioners; recall notices; public-interest journalism; or a patient's private discussion with a health practitioner.

C. Independent medicines information service

Advertising removal must not create an information vacuum.

An independent publicly funded medicines-information service should provide plain-language information on expected benefits; known risks; uncertainty; alternative treatments; non-pharmaceutical options where clinically relevant; funding status; conflicts of interest in the supporting research; and how to report an adverse event.

Medsafe already provides access to medicine data sheets and consumer medicine information, while Pharmac publishes the Pharmaceutical Schedule of funded medicines.[5]

The policy would integrate and expand these functions without placing pharmaceutical promotion inside the public-information service.

D. Transition

A 12-month transition should apply to existing advertising contracts.

Government should publish an annual report showing enforcement action; breaches; expenditure on independent medicines information; changes in prescribing requests; prescribing patterns; health outcomes where measurable; and effects on media revenue.

V. POLICY THREE: MEDIA REVENUE TRANSPARENCY

The Purchased Silence Problem cannot be tested without financial information.

Parliament should create a Media Revenue Transparency Register administered by an independent officer of Parliament or an institution protected from ministerial direction.

A. Covered entities

The register should apply to news organisations exceeding a threshold based on annual New Zealand audience; annual revenue; public funding received; or national or regional market share.

B. Required disclosures

Covered organisations should disclose annually:

1. total advertising and sponsorship revenue;

2. revenue by industrial sector;

3. any advertiser or associated corporate group supplying more than 1 per cent of annual revenue;

4. government advertising revenue;

5. political advertising revenue;

6. sponsored editorial or native advertising;

7. significant advertiser withdrawals;

8. the organisation's editorial-commercial separation policy;

9. ownership and beneficial ownership;

10. related-party transactions; and

11. the number of substantiated internal complaints concerning commercial interference.

Individual campaign pricing could remain confidential unless necessary to investigate a statutory breach.

C. Withdrawal notices

Where an advertiser withdraws a material account within 90 days of critical reporting concerning that advertiser, both parties should file a confidential notice with the regulator.

The notice would not presume wrongdoing.

It would create an auditable record from which patterns could later be identified.

D. Editorial independence certification

The governing board of each covered media organisation should certify annually that editorial staff retain final editorial authority; sponsored content is clearly labelled; journalists may report attempted interference; retaliation is prohibited; and the board has reviewed material advertiser complaints.

E. Public-interest test

The regulator would publish aggregated findings rather than making insinuations from isolated events.

The purpose is measurement, not accusation.

VI. POLICY FOUR: A PUBLIC-INTEREST JOURNALISM TRUST

Advertising transparency alone will not resolve the economic weakness of journalism.

A Public-Interest Journalism Trust should be established by statute.

A. Independence

The Trust should be governed through staggered appointments involving Parliament; the Auditor-General; the Ombudsman; universities; journalism bodies; Māori media representatives; regional communities; and members selected through an open public process.

No sitting Minister, Member of Parliament, party officer or active corporate lobbyist should serve as a trustee.

B. Funding

Funding should come from a diversified base: a parliamentary appropriation; a levy on dominant digital advertising intermediaries; a portion of regulatory penalties; voluntary donations subject to disclosure; and returns from a protected endowment.

No donor should be permitted to direct editorial outcomes.

C. Eligible work

The Trust would support local-government reporting; court reporting; investigative journalism; scientific and health journalism; Māori and Pacific journalism; rural and provincial reporting; public-data projects; long-form policy investigation; and journalism in communities without sustainable commercial coverage.

D. Publication independence

Grants would fund capacity, not conclusions.

Recipients would retain editorial independence and publish their methodology, sources where lawful, corrections policy and material conflicts.

E. Open archive

Work substantially funded by the Trust should enter a free public archive after a reasonable first-publication period.

Public money would thereby create a permanent public information asset.

VII. POLICY FIVE: THE LOBBYING AND INFLUENCE REGISTER

New Zealand should enact a comprehensive Lobbying and Influence Transparency Act.

A. Registration

Professional lobbyists and organisations undertaking material lobbying should register clients; subject areas; responsible personnel; relevant former public offices; broad expenditure bands; and controlling interests.

B. Ministerial and senior-official diaries

Ministers, chief executives and designated senior officials should publish monthly records of substantive meetings concerning legislation; regulation; public procurement; major public assets; subsidies; tax concessions; resource allocation; and licensing.

Personal casework and protected security matters could be exempted.

C. Written influence record

Where a lobbyist supplies draft legislative language, regulatory wording, economic modelling or public-communications material, that contribution should be identified in the departmental record.

D. Cooling-off periods

Former Ministers, senior political advisers, regulators and departmental chief executives should face proportionate cooling-off periods before undertaking paid lobbying in fields for which they recently held direct responsibility.

E. Citizen equality statement

Every major legislative proposal should include an influence statement showing organised interests consulted; public submissions received; meetings held; expert evidence commissioned; funding sources of commissioned research; and groups materially affected but not represented.

This would reveal the missing voice as well as the present voice.

VIII. POLICY SIX: GAMBLING HARM AND THE HARM DIVIDEND PARADOX

New Zealand recorded total gambling expenditure of approximately $2.792 billion for the 2023/24 reporting period across TAB betting, lotteries, non-casino gaming machines and casinos.[6]

The current national strategy for preventing and minimising gambling harm covers the three-year period beginning 1 July 2025.[7]

Solar System Jurisprudence proposes that gambling policy be governed by the principle:

No community benefit should depend upon concealing or externalising the household harm required to generate it.

A. Gambling loss statements

Every licensed operator should provide clear periodic statements showing money deposited; money wagered; money withdrawn; net loss; time spent; credit used where applicable; and available exclusion and treatment services.

B. Advertising restrictions

Gambling advertising should be prohibited during programmes primarily directed at children; near schools; through paid influencers appealing substantially to minors; through inducements that obscure likely loss; through personalised targeting of self-excluded or high-risk users; and during live sport except within narrowly controlled information windows.

C. Community funding reform

Community organisations should be progressively moved away from dependency upon gaming-machine proceeds.

A transition fund should replace a declining proportion of gaming-derived grants with general revenue over ten years.

This would permit community institutions to survive without becoming advocates for the continuation of harmful revenue machinery.

D. Local sinking-lid powers

Local authorities should retain and strengthen powers to reduce non-casino gaming-machine venues and machines.

Communities carrying the social costs should possess meaningful control over machine density.

E. Harm-based levy

Operator levies should reflect measured risk.

Higher-risk products, machine designs and marketing practices should bear higher levies.

F. Online gambling controls

The Online Casino Gambling Act 2026 created a regulated online-casino regime that is being implemented progressively, with full operation not expected until 2027.[8]

Licensing should include enforceable loss limits; a national exclusion register; intervention duties; transaction monitoring; restrictions on inducements; clear data access for regulators; New Zealand dispute resolution; prohibition of credit-based gambling; mandatory contribution to treatment and research; and strong sanctions for unlawful advertising.

IX. POLICY SEVEN: HOUSING AS CIVIC INFRASTRUCTURE

Severe housing deprivation is not merely a welfare issue.

It affects health, education, justice, productivity, family stability, democratic participation and intergenerational opportunity.

HUD reported that 67.9 per cent of people estimated to be severely housing deprived in 2023 had lived in the same place for a year, indicating that deprivation was not merely temporary for many affected people.[9]

Solar System Jurisprudence therefore treats secure housing as civic infrastructure.

A. National housing floor

Central government should establish measurable minimum objectives for reduction in unsheltered homelessness; reduction in severe housing deprivation; emergency-housing duration; healthy-home compliance; social-housing waiting times; and housing affordability relative to household income.

B. Housing First expansion

Long-term homelessness should ordinarily be addressed through stable housing accompanied by appropriate support, rather than repeated movement through temporary accommodation.

C. Public land register

A national register should identify unused or underused Crown land; local-authority land; transport-adjacent land; land held for obsolete purposes; infrastructure capacity; and suitability for housing.

D. Intergenerational housing fund

A protected fund should finance social housing; community housing; papakāinga; supported housing; accessible housing; transitional housing; and infrastructure opening land for residential use.

E. Empty and speculative property data

Government should measure persistent vacancy, land banking and short-term accommodation concentration before imposing policy responses.

Again, measurement must precede accusation.

F. Housing impact statements

Major tax, immigration, infrastructure and land-use decisions should include a housing impact statement examining effects on rents; house prices; construction capacity; public housing demand; regional distribution; and homelessness risk.

X. POLICY EIGHT: CHILDREN, YOUTH AND CRIME PREVENTION

Solar System Jurisprudence rejects the false choice between accountability and prevention.

A state may restrain serious offending while also addressing the conditions from which offending repeatedly emerges.

Research by the Office of the Prime Minister's Chief Science Advisor has emphasised that youth offending prevention requires early, sustained and evidence-informed intervention rather than reliance upon a single late-stage response.[10]

A. Early-life guarantee

Every child should have effective access to antenatal and infant health services; developmental screening; early-childhood education; school attendance support; primary healthcare; dental care; safe housing; family-violence intervention; and mental-health and addiction support.

B. School re-engagement teams

Persistent non-attendance should trigger a coordinated response involving family or whānau; school; health services; housing agencies; youth services; and, where necessary, justice agencies.

C. Youth employment covenant

Government procurement should create supervised pathways into trades; conservation work; digital services; infrastructure; horticulture; transport; energy; community care; and emergency resilience.

D. Serious offending

Serious violent and organised offending must attract proportionate investigation, prosecution and sentence.

However, success should be measured not only by arrests and imprisonment, but by victimisation reduction; desistance; school participation; stable housing; employment; addiction treatment; family safety; and reduced recruitment into criminal organisations.

E. Criminal wealth recovery

Recovered proceeds of organised crime should be transparently allocated among victim support; addiction treatment; youth prevention; community safety; financial investigation; and independent evaluation.

XI. POLICY NINE: PUBLIC ASSETS AND INTERGENERATIONAL TRUSTEESHIP

The sale of a public asset is not an ordinary disposal.

It may transfer monopoly power; strategic capacity; land; water access; data; infrastructure; future dividends; development rights; and control over essential services.

New Zealand's Public Finance Act 1989 establishes principles and reporting structures for responsible fiscal management.[11]

Solar System Jurisprudence would add an explicit principle of intergenerational asset stewardship.

A. Strategic asset classification

Assets should be classified as ordinary commercial assets; essential service assets; natural monopoly assets; strategic land or infrastructure; taonga or culturally significant assets; national resilience assets; or intergenerational natural-resource assets.

B. Disposal threshold

A strategic public asset should not be sold unless an independent valuation is published; future income foregone is calculated; monopoly and competition effects are assessed; Māori rights and interests are addressed; national-security and resilience consequences are examined; alternatives are published; the Auditor-General reviews the process; and Parliament approves the disposal through an enhanced procedure.

C. Enhanced approval

For assets exceeding a statutory threshold, approval should require a 60 per cent vote of the House; or a simple parliamentary majority followed by approval at referendum.

Because one Parliament generally cannot permanently prevent a later Parliament from legislating, the protection would operate as a strong statutory procedure and political commitment rather than an absolute constitutional barrier.

D. Proceeds

Proceeds from strategic asset sales should not fund ordinary operating expenditure.

They should be applied to debt reduction; replacement productive assets; resilience infrastructure; or a protected intergenerational fund.

E. Public balance-sheet reporting

Budgets should show not merely annual cash effects, but changes in public net worth; strategic capability; future dividend streams; natural capital; infrastructure condition; contingent liabilities; and intergenerational distribution.

XII. POLICY TEN: THE AOTEAROA INTERGENERATIONAL RESOURCE TRUST

Solar System Jurisprudence holds that finite natural resources are not created by the present generation.

The present generation may use them, but should not treat the entire value as if it arose solely from present effort.

An Aotearoa Intergenerational Resource Trust should receive a statutory share of net public revenue arising from Crown minerals; seabed resources where lawfully developed; spectrum auctions; carbon-market windfalls; major water-use concessions where charges apply; space-resource interests acquired by New Zealand; penalties for serious environmental damage; and other finite public-resource rents.

A. The one per cent principle

As an initial Solar System Jurisprudence rule, at least 1 per cent of specified gross public-resource receipts should be transferred to the Trust before ordinary distribution.

Parliament could prescribe higher resource-specific rates.

The 1 per cent is a constitutional seed, not a claim that every resource can be governed identically.

B. Beneficiaries

The Trust would be held for present New Zealanders; future New Zealanders; ecological restoration; national resilience; scientific knowledge; Māori interests recognised by law and Te Tiriti principles; and peaceful international cooperation.

C. Investment

The Trust should invest for long-term real returns subject to ethical investment rules; climate and transition-risk reporting; public disclosure; independent audit; limits on political direction; and preservation of capital.

D. Permitted distributions

Distributions should support long-duration public goods such as science; preventive health; biodiversity; disaster resilience; housing infrastructure; education endowments; clean energy; public data; and future space-law capability.

XIII. POLICY ELEVEN: SOLAR SYSTEM GOVERNANCE CAPABILITY

New Zealand need not wait until large-scale off-world mining exists before developing law for it.

The peaceful and lawful use of space will increasingly involve questions of satellites; spectrum; orbital congestion; debris; remote sensing; launch liability; planetary protection; resource extraction; data ownership; military use; environmental responsibility; and benefit sharing.

A. Solar System Jurisprudence Commission

A multidisciplinary commission should be established involving public law; international law; tikanga Māori; astronomy; engineering; environmental science; economics; ethics; defence; telecommunications; insurance; and future-generations analysis.

B. Functions

The Commission would:

1. review New Zealand's space and spectrum laws;

2. advise on international negotiations;

3. develop principles for responsible resource use;

4. consider orbital environmental standards;

5. design liability and insurance rules;

6. examine benefit-sharing models;

7. protect scientific and cultural interests;

8. publish accessible public reports; and

9. maintain a register of significant New Zealand space interests.

C. Proposed foundational rule

No actor acquires an unlimited moral entitlement merely by being first to reach, identify or commercially exploit a finite extraterrestrial resource.

Lawful commercial activity may be recognised.

But a share of the benefit should support common scientific knowledge; environmental protection; peaceful use; emergency capability; and future generations.

XIV. POLICY TWELVE: DEMOCRATIC DISTRIBUTION AND LOCAL VOICE

A healthy national system cannot be controlled entirely from the centre.

Local communities should possess sufficient authority, information and revenue to respond to local needs.

A. Participatory budgeting

Councils should allocate a defined portion of discretionary capital expenditure through participatory budgeting.

Residents would propose and rank local projects under transparent fiscal limits.

B. Citizens' panels

Major long-term issues may be referred to randomly selected citizens' panels supplied with balanced expert evidence; competing submissions; conflict disclosures; independent facilitation; and adequate time for deliberation.

The panel would advise rather than replace elected representatives.

C. Public forum rights

Councils and specified Crown bodies should maintain accessible public-forum procedures with published speaking rules; remote participation; disability access; timely responses; public records; and safeguards against viewpoint discrimination.

D. Civic data standard

Public information should be searchable; downloadable; machine-readable where practicable; understandable on a mobile phone; available without compulsory commercial sign-in; archived; and accompanied by definitions and methodology.

Information technically published but practically unusable is only partially public.

XV. THE SOLAR SYSTEM JURISPRUDENCE IMPACT STATEMENT

Every Bill and major regulation should include a Solar System Jurisprudence Impact Statement.

The statement would ask:

1. Gravity: Where is power concentrated?

2. Voice: Who can influence the decision and who cannot?

3. Orbit: What indirect consequences will follow?

4. Time: Who benefits now and who pays later?

5. Resource: Is a finite or inherited asset being consumed?

6. Externality: Which costs fall outside the immediate transaction?

7. Resilience: Does the proposal increase or reduce national capacity?

8. Transparency: Can the public inspect the evidence?

9. Reversibility: Can the decision be corrected if it fails?

10. Future generations: What remains after the present beneficiaries are gone?

11. Te Tiriti and tikanga: What Māori rights, interests, relationships and responsibilities are engaged?

12. Planetary responsibility: Does the policy create effects beyond New Zealand's territory or generation?

The statement would not mechanically determine the result.

Its function would be to compel a wider field of vision.

XVI. INSTITUTIONAL DESIGN

The policy suite should not be administered by one all-powerful ministry.

That would merely create a new centre of gravity.

Responsibility should be distributed.

A. Parliament

Parliament would enact the framework and receive annual reports.

B. Officers of Parliament

The Auditor-General and Ombudsman would retain and strengthen oversight functions relevant to public finance, information and administrative conduct.

C. Independent regulators

Specialist regulators would oversee medicine advertising; media revenue transparency; lobbying; gambling; privacy; competition; and space activity.

D. Courts

Courts would review legality, procedural fairness, proportionality and statutory compliance.

They would not become editors of political truth.

E. Civil society and universities

Independent research institutions should audit the effects of the reforms and identify unintended consequences.

F. Māori participation

Institutional design should provide meaningful Māori participation consistent with the particular statutory function, applicable Treaty settlements, legal rights and the Crown's broader constitutional responsibilities.

XVII. IMPLEMENTATION PROGRAMME

A. First 100 days

The Government should:

1. introduce legislation prohibiting direct-to-consumer prescription-medicine advertising;

2. establish an interim Media Revenue Transparency Working Group;

3. publish a lobbying-register exposure draft;

4. direct agencies to prepare Statements of Public Reasoning for major new initiatives;

5. begin a public land and strategic asset inventory;

6. commission baseline studies of media revenue, gambling harm and severe housing deprivation;

7. establish the Solar System Jurisprudence Commission;

8. publish drafting instructions for the Intergenerational Resource Trust; and

9. create an implementation website with open documents and public submissions.

B. First year

Parliament should enact the Informed National Voice Act; the Prescription Medicines Advertising Reform Act; the Media Revenue Transparency Act; the Lobbying and Influence Transparency Act; and enabling legislation for the Public-Interest Journalism Trust.

Government should also begin the community gambling-funding transition; expansion of Housing First; youth re-engagement teams; strategic asset classification; and the national civic-data standard.

C. Years two and three

Government should implement the Intergenerational Resource Trust; enhanced asset-disposal procedures; participatory budgeting pilots; national gambling-loss statements; media-sector annual reporting; open public-interest journalism archives; integrated youth prevention outcomes; and the first national Solar System Jurisprudence Impact Report.

D. Five-year review

An independent review should consider democratic participation; trust in public institutions; media diversity; health-information quality; prescription patterns; gambling harm; homelessness; youth offending; organised-crime recruitment; public net worth; regulatory burden; freedom of expression; unintended market concentration; and compliance costs.

XVIII. FISCAL DISCIPLINE

A policy suite without financing is rhetoric.

The programme should therefore operate under four fiscal rules.

Rule One: identify the payer

Every proposal must identify whether it is funded by general taxation; a sector levy; reprioritisation; recovered proceeds; investment returns; borrowing; user charges; or reduced expenditure elsewhere.

Rule Two: match levy to harm or benefit

A levy should bear a rational relationship to harm created; regulatory cost; privilege granted; scarcity value; or commercial benefit received.

Rule Three: protect capital

Intergenerational funds should not be used as ordinary operating accounts.

Rule Four: publish opportunity cost

Every significant expenditure should identify what alternative use of public money is displaced.

Solar System Jurisprudence does not abolish scarcity.

It makes scarcity visible.

XIX. SAFEGUARDS AGAINST OVERREACH

A programme designed to expose power can itself become powerful.

It therefore requires safeguards.

A. No ministry of truth

No agency should possess general authority to determine which political opinion is true.

B. Judicial review

Regulatory decisions should be subject to judicial review and, where appropriate, statutory appeal.

C. Proportionality

Reporting duties should be proportionate to institutional size and public influence.

D. Protection of confidential sources

Media transparency must not expose confidential journalistic sources.

E. Commercial confidentiality

Legitimate commercial confidentiality may be protected, but not used to conceal material influence over public decisions.

F. Privacy

Disclosure systems should avoid unnecessary publication of private personal information.

G. Sunset and review clauses

New regulatory powers should be reviewed after five years.

H. Parliamentary scrutiny

Significant secondary legislation should be reviewable by Parliament and subject to the Regulations Review Committee.

XX. CONCLUSION

New Zealand does not require a theory that everyone is corrupt.

It requires institutions capable of operating safely even when some actors are self-interested, some information is incomplete and some power is concentrated.

The purpose of Solar System Jurisprudence is not to replace all existing law with astronomical metaphor.

Its purpose is to correct a recurring legal blindness.

Traditional law frequently examines the immediate act: the advertisement; the contract; the sale; the bet; the eviction; the arrest; the licence; the asset transfer.

Solar System Jurisprudence examines the field around the act: who supplied the money; who controlled the information; who carried the risk; who received the gain; who remained unheard; what moved in consequence; what became irreversible; and what was left for the next generation.

The national policy suite therefore rests upon a simple sequence:

Measure the gravity.

Disclose the gravity.

Distribute the voice.

Protect the social floor.

Preserve the inherited asset.

Account for the future.

The nation is not merely a market operating during the present financial year.

It is an intergenerational vessel.

Its institutions are navigation systems.

Its laws determine who may steer, who may see the chart, who must absorb the storm and whether anything remains when the present crew has left the deck.

That is the task of Solar System Jurisprudence in Aotearoa New Zealand.

Public-Interest Journalism Trust Act — to create an independent statutory trust funding public-interest reporting without ministerial editorial control.

Intergenerational Resource Trust Act — to preserve a statutory share of finite public-resource revenue for future generations, resilience, science and ecological restoration.

Strategic Public Assets Protection Act — to require valuation, public reasoning, enhanced parliamentary approval and intergenerational accounting before disposal of strategic public assets.
